## Question 1 — Delay and fortuitous event

### Record metadata

- `bar_year`: `2024`
- `exam_date`: `2024-09-08` (pending provenance review above)
- `subject`: `Civil Law`
- `question_number`: `1`
- `source_page`: `2` (PDF page; questionnaire question page is unnumbered)
- `review_status`: `unreviewed`
- `locked`: `false`

### Exact question text

Carlos and Chloe scheduled their intimate wedding on September 4, 2024 at 7:00 p.m. in Manila.
Chloe purchased from Angelica a gold wedding ring with engraving “Love, Carlos” for PHP
1,000,000.00. Angelica promised to deliver the ring on or before the wedding day. On September 1,
2024, a weather bulletin warned that super typhoon Enteng will enter the country within three days.
On September 4, 2024 at 6:00 a.m., typhoon Enteng and the enhanced southwest monsoon caused massive
flooding that rendered major roads in Manila impassable to all kinds of vehicles. Angelica failed to
deliver the ring in time for Carlos and Chloe’s wedding which pushed through despite the typhoon.
Angelica delivered the ring the following day after the flood subsided. Chloe filed against Angelica
a complaint for damages. Angelica denied liability and argued that typhoon is a fortuitous event.
Chloe replied that typhoon is not a fortuitous event because its arrival was earlier announced. In
any event, the typhoon will not exempt Angelica because of her negligence and default. Angelica was
negligent when she failed to pay attention to the forecast and was in default when she delivered the
ring after the wedding. Is Angelica liable for damages due to default in complying with her
obligation? Explain.

### Draft answer key

- **Direct answer:** Yes, subject to proof that the announced storm gave Angelica a reasonable
  opportunity to avoid nonperformance by delivering before the wedding day.
- **Legal basis:** Civil Code Articles 1169, 1170, 1173, and 1174. Demand is unnecessary when the time
  of performance was a controlling motive. A foreseeable event may still be fortuitous if
  unavoidable, but the obligor must be free from negligence or contribution to the loss.
- **Application:** The engraved wedding ring and fixed wedding date made timely delivery essential.
  The September 1 warning gave Angelica notice before the September 4 flooding, yet the facts state
  no precaution or attempted early delivery. Her omission supports negligence and prevents reliance
  on fortuitous event, even though the actual flooding made same-day travel impossible.
- **Conclusion:** Angelica may be held liable for damages caused by late delivery.
- **Defensible alternative:** No liability may be accepted if the answer explains that delivery was
  not yet due before September 4 and proves the flooding was inevitable, made performance impossible,
  and was not aggravated by Angelica. The forecast alone does not make an unavoidable event
  non-fortuitous.
- **Authorities to verify:** Civil Code Arts. 1169, 1170, 1173, 1174; *Philippine Communications
  Satellite Corp. v. Globe Telecom, Inc.*, G.R. No. 147324, May 25, 2004.
- **Common errors:** Treating foreseeability alone as conclusive; omitting the no-demand exception;
  assuming every typhoon automatically excuses performance; failing to analyze Angelica's conduct
  between September 1 and September 4.

## Question 2 — Unauthorized sale through an agent

### Record metadata

- `bar_year`: `2024`; `exam_date`: `2024-09-08`; `subject`: `Civil Law`
- `question_number`: `2`; `source_page`: `2`; `review_status`: `unreviewed`; `locked`: `false`

### Exact question text

Viada and Blanco are best friends. In July 2024, Blanco sold to Castan a parcel of land registered in
Viada’s name. Blanco signed the deed of absolute sale in behalf of Viada and pretended as his agent.
In August 2024, Viada executed a special power of attorney (SPA) authorizing Blanco to sell the
identical parcel of land. Blanco executed a deed of absolute sale in favor of Sanchez Roman who was
aware of the prior conveyance of the same land to Castan. Thereafter, Castan learned of the subsequent
transaction between Blanco and Sanchez Roman. Castan then filed against Sanchez Roman an action for
annulment of sale. Castan argued that Sanchez Roman is in bad faith because of his knowledge of the
prior sale. Sanchez Roman countered that the sale between Blanco and Castan is void for lack of
Viada’s consent. Castan replied that the subsequent issuance of the SPA to Blanco ratified any defect
in their transaction. Who between Castan and Sanchez Roman has a better right over the parcel of
land? Explain.

### Draft answer key

- **Direct answer:** Sanchez Roman has the better right.
- **Legal basis:** Under Civil Code Articles 1874 and 1878(5), an agent's authority to sell land must
  be in writing; otherwise the sale is void. A void contract cannot be ratified under Articles 1409
  and 1410. The double-sale rule does not elevate a buyer under a void first sale.
- **Application:** Blanco had no written authority when he purported to sell Viada's land to Castan
  in July. The August SPA authorized a sale prospectively but did not expressly confirm the earlier
  deed, and a void land sale cannot be cured merely by later authority. Blanco possessed written
  authority when he sold to Sanchez Roman. Knowledge of Castan's void transaction does not make that
  transaction valid or defeat Viada's authorized sale.
- **Conclusion:** Castan cannot annul the authorized sale; Sanchez Roman has the better right.
- **Defensible alternative:** Castan prevails only if the later instrument and Viada's acts are found
  to be an express written confirmation or adoption of the July sale, rather than merely prospective
  authority. The reviewer must decide whether that interpretation is supportable on the stated facts.
- **Authorities to verify:** Civil Code Arts. 1874, 1878(5), 1409, 1410; *Escueta v. Lim*, G.R. No.
  137162, January 24, 2007; *Dizon v. Court of Appeals*, G.R. No. 122544, January 28, 1999.
- **Common errors:** Applying Article 1544 without first testing validity; treating an SPA as automatic
  ratification; deciding solely on Sanchez Roman's bad faith.

## Question 3 — Reconveyance, implied trust, and possession

### Record metadata

- `bar_year`: `2024`; `exam_date`: `2024-09-08`; `subject`: `Civil Law`
- `question_number`: `3`; `source_page`: `2` (continues on PDF page 3); `review_status`: `unreviewed`; `locked`: `false`

### Exact question text

Alice and Sheila occupied a parcel of land which they inherited from their parents. In 2003, Alice
executed an affidavit of self-adjudication over the property and was issued a certificate of title
solely under her name. In August 2024, Sheila discovered that she was excluded from the certificate
of title. Sheila filed against Alice an action to reconvey the land based on implied trust. Sheila
alleged continuous actual possession of the property and deprivation of share as compulsory heir.
Alice countered that the prescriptive period to reconvey real property based on an implied trust is
10 years reckoned when she repudiated the trust in 2003 upon the issuance of certificate of title in
her name. Thus, Sheila’s action for reconveyance filed only in 2024 had already prescribed. Is
Sheila’s cause of action to recover her share in the property from Alice already prescribed?
Explain.

### Draft answer key

- **Direct answer:** No. Sheila's action has not prescribed while she remains in actual possession.
- **Legal basis:** An action to reconvey property based on an implied trust ordinarily prescribes in
  ten years from registration when the claimant is not in possession. When the claimant remains in
  possession, the action is effectively one to quiet title and is imprescriptible while possession
  continues. Co-ownership also requires a clear, communicated repudiation before prescription runs.
- **Application:** Sheila continuously occupied the inherited land. Alice's title did not place
  Sheila out of possession, and Sheila discovered the exclusion only in August 2024. On these facts,
  mere registration in Alice's sole name did not make Sheila's possessory claim prescribe.
- **Conclusion:** Sheila may pursue recovery of her hereditary share.
- **Defensible alternative:** Prescription may run from an earlier date only if Alice proves a clear,
  unequivocal repudiation of the co-ownership or trust that was communicated to Sheila, plus the
  required lapse of time. Those facts are not stated.
- **Authorities to verify:** Civil Code Arts. 494, 1456; *Heirs of Clemente Ermac v. Heirs of Vicente
  Ermac*, G.R. No. 149679, May 30, 2003; *Viloria v. Court of Appeals*, G.R. No. 119974, June 30,
  1999.
- **Common errors:** Automatically counting ten years from registration; ignoring continuous
  possession; treating registration alone as conclusive repudiation between co-heirs.

## Question 4 — Assignment of lease and novation

### Record metadata

- `bar_year`: `2024`; `exam_date`: `2024-09-08`; `subject`: `Civil Law`
- `question_number`: `4`; `source_page`: `3`; `review_status`: `unreviewed`; `locked`: `false`

### Exact question text

In 2017, Maloi leased her real property to Sheena for a period of 10 years with rental escalation
clause which reads: “For the first five years, the rental rate shall be PHP 10,000.00 per month
subject to 10% increase for the succeeding years.” In 2020, Sheena assigned to Colet her rights and
obligations under the contract of lease. Colet then paid the agreed rentals to Maloi. In 2023, Colet
paid PHP 10,000.00 monthly rent through postdated checks but were dishonored for having been drawn
against a closed account. Maloi charged Colet with the crime of estafa. On the other hand, Maloi
demanded from Sheena the unpaid rentals and stipulated increase in rent but was refused. Sheena
denied liability and argued that her obligation was already extinguished when Colet substituted her
as lessee. Sheena added that Maloi accepted rental payments from Colet. Did novation extinguish
Sheena’s obligation under the contract of lease? Explain.

### Draft answer key

- **Direct answer:** No. Sheena's obligation was not extinguished by novation.
- **Legal basis:** Civil Code Articles 1291–1293 require the creditor's consent to substitution of a
  debtor and a clear intent to extinguish the old obligation. Novation is never presumed. Article
  1649 requires the lessor's consent to assignment unless the lease provides otherwise. Acceptance
  of payment from a third person does not, by itself, unequivocally release the original debtor.
- **Application:** Maloi accepted Colet's rent, which may show consent to Colet's performance, but no
  fact shows that Maloi expressly or unmistakably released Sheena. The original lease and Colet's
  assumed performance can coexist. Maloi's later demand against Sheena is inconsistent with an intent
  to release her.
- **Conclusion:** Sheena remains answerable under the lease, subject to credit for payments actually
  received from Colet and the contract's escalation clause.
- **Defensible alternative:** Novation may be found only if additional evidence shows Maloi's clear
  and unequivocal consent to substitute Colet and discharge Sheena—not merely acceptance of rent.
- **Authorities to verify:** Civil Code Arts. 1291, 1292, 1293, 1649; *Romago, Inc. v. Associated
  Bank*, G.R. No. 223450, February 22, 2023; *Ajax Marketing and Development Corp. v. Court of
  Appeals*, G.R. No. 118585, September 14, 1995.
- **Common errors:** Equating assignment with novation; inferring release solely from accepted rent;
  discussing estafa instead of the civil-law issue asked.

## Question 5 — Foreign decedent's holographic will

### Record metadata

- `bar_year`: `2024`; `exam_date`: `2024-09-08`; `subject`: `Civil Law`
- `question_number`: `5`; `source_page`: `3`; `review_status`: `unreviewed`; `locked`: `false`

### Exact question text

Primo, a French national and permanent resident of Canada, executed in the Philippines a holographic
will which he entirely wrote, dated and signed. Primo enumerated in the will various real and
personal properties situated in the Philippines, France and Canada. Primo bequeathed the properties
in the Philippines to his parents, the properties in France to his siblings, and the properties in
Canada to his wife and children. In 2024, Primo suffered a heart attack and died in Australia while
attending a conference. Primo was survived by his wife, two children, parents, and three siblings.
What laws shall govern the formalities of Primo’s holographic will, the distribution of his estate,
and the qualifications of his heirs? Explain.

### Draft answer key

- **Direct answer:** The will's formal validity may be governed by Philippine law or the law of
  Primo's nationality; French law governs succession to the estate and capacity to inherit.
- **Legal basis:** Civil Code Articles 17, 810, and 817 govern testamentary form. Article 817 permits
  an alien's will made in the Philippines to follow the law of the alien's country; Philippine law is
  also available for extrinsic validity. Under Article 16, the decedent's national law governs the
  order of succession, successional rights, and intrinsic validity regardless of the property's
  nature or location. Article 1039 likewise refers capacity to succeed to the decedent's national law.
- **Application:** Primo executed the will in the Philippines and complied on the stated facts with
  Philippine holographic-will essentials by entirely writing, dating, and signing it. French law may
  alternatively validate its form if pleaded and proved. Because Primo remained French, French
  succession law—not Canadian residence, Australian place of death, or each property's situs—governs
  distribution and heir qualifications under Philippine conflict rules.
- **Conclusion:** Philippine or proven French law governs formalities; French law governs intrinsic
  distribution and the heirs' capacity to succeed.
- **Defensible alternative:** A renvoi analysis may be credited if the answer accurately establishes
  the relevant French conflicts rule and explains why it refers the matter elsewhere. Foreign law
  must be pleaded and proved; otherwise Philippine courts generally apply processual presumption.
- **Authorities to verify:** Civil Code Arts. 16, 17, 810, 817, 1039; *In re Estate of Johnson*, G.R.
  No. 229010, November 23, 2020; *Bellis v. Bellis*, G.R. No. L-23678, June 6, 1967.
- **Common errors:** Applying lex rei sitae to intrinsic succession; using Canadian domicile as the
  governing personal law; applying Australian law merely because death occurred there; confusing
  probate jurisdiction with substantive succession law.
